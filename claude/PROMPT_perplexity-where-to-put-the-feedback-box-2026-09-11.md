===== PROMPT START =====

Research question: on a public information website, where on the page should a "report a problem / leave a note" control sit so that real, useful reports actually arrive, and what wording gets people to use it?

Answer from published research and measured results, not from opinion pieces.

THE SITUATION THIS IS FOR:
- A free, fan-made reference website about a video game. It lists ships, their prices, and where to buy them in the game.
- Visitors are looking things up, not filling in forms. Most visits are short.
- The main page is a searchable list of about 250 items, each shown as a card with a few facts and prices on it. There are also individual pages for each item.
- Some of the facts are wrong or out of date, and the visitor looking at a card is often the person best placed to say so.
- Traffic is low. There is no login, and no way to email visitors back.

WHAT I WANT ANSWERED:
1. Placement. Where do reports actually come from: a control next to the individual item, a control fixed to the edge of the screen, one at the end of the page, one in the navigation, or something else? Rank them by evidence, and say plainly which are weakest.
2. Item-level versus page-level. Is a "this is wrong" control attached to each item worth the clutter compared with one feedback box for the whole page? Is there measured evidence either way?
3. Wording. What is measured about the words on the control and on its heading — "report a problem", "something wrong?", "suggest a correction", "feedback" — including anything on how the wording changes who replies and what they send.
4. Fields. How much does asking for more fields reduce the number of reports, and which single field earns its place? Say what the research shows about optional email fields when no reply is promised.
5. What kills it. Practices that measurably lower response or annoy visitors: pop-ups, timed prompts, floating side tabs, leaving the site to a third-party form, asking to rate the page.
6. Phones. What changes at phone width, including how big the control has to be and where on screen a thumb can reach.
7. After the send. What to show once a note is sent, and whether saying what happens to the report changes how many people send one.
8. Low traffic. With very few visitors, how do you tell whether the feedback route is working at all? What do people use instead of counting submissions?

RULES FOR YOUR ANSWER:
- Label every claim: EVIDENCE (a study, an experiment, or published measured results — name the source and its date), WEAK EVIDENCE (practitioner guidance from a recognised usability or research organisation, no numbers), or OPINION (yours).
- Prefer primary sources and recognised usability research organisations. Say when a source is a vendor selling a feedback product, because that is an interested party.
- Say plainly when the research does not answer a question, rather than filling the gap.
- Note the age of each source. Say which findings come from before mobile-first design and may not carry over.
- Give me the ranked recommendation first, then the evidence behind it.
- No code and no design mockups.
